## Document Purpose

The Hamkke Teaching Framework defines the educational principles and teaching standards that guide lessons at Hamkke.

This framework applies to live online English lessons delivered through Hamkke, across different lesson durations, learner ages, English levels, and learning goals.

It exists to ensure that learners receive a consistent educational experience across Hamkke while allowing teachers to bring their own personality, professional judgment, and teaching strengths into the classroom.

The framework is not a fixed lesson script.

It provides a shared approach to how Hamkke teachers listen, ask questions, respond to learners, teach language, give feedback, and create opportunities for learners to use English meaningfully.

As Hamkke grows, this framework serves as a foundation for:

- lesson delivery
- teacher onboarding and training
- learner progress assessment
- Progress Reports
- teaching quality and consistency
- development of learning materials and resources
- future educational programs and services

## Our Educational Belief

At Hamkke, we believe that learning English and being able to use English are not always the same thing.

A learner may understand grammar, know vocabulary, complete exercises, or read well and still find it difficult to express a thought naturally in conversation.

For this reason, Hamkke does not treat conversation simply as a way to practice English after learning it.

**Conversation is part of how learning happens.**

Through meaningful interaction, learners discover what they can already express, where their communication becomes difficult, what language they need, and how they can express their thoughts more clearly.

The role of the teacher is not simply to provide more English.

The teacher helps learners make better use of the English they already have, develop the language they need next, and gradually become more independent communicators.

## The Hamkke Approach

The Hamkke Approach is a conversation-centered teaching framework designed to help learners move from knowing English to using it meaningfully and independently.

The approach follows a recurring four-stage cycle:

### 1. We Talk

**Start with the learner, not the lesson.**

Begin with genuine communication and give the learner space to express what they already know.

The teacher listens before teaching, paying attention to how the learner currently communicates, where they hesitate, what they can already express, and what they may need help expressing.

**Teacher goal:** Understand how this learner currently communicates.

### 2. We Go Deeper

**Help the learner say more of what they mean.**

Build naturally from the learner's answers through relevant follow-up questions.

Encourage learners to explain, describe, clarify, give reasons, provide examples, compare ideas, reflect, and express opinions at a level appropriate for them.

Teachers follow the learner's thoughts rather than mechanically moving through prepared questions.

**Teacher goal:** Stretch the learner's communication without making the conversation feel like a test.

### 3. We Refine

**Improve the English without losing the person behind it.**

Use language that naturally emerges from conversation as an opportunity for learning.

Teachers help learners improve useful vocabulary, grammar, pronunciation, sentence structure, and natural expression while protecting the flow of communication.

Not every mistake needs to become a correction.

Teachers prioritize feedback that will meaningfully help the learner communicate more clearly, naturally, or independently.

**Teacher goal:** Give the learner better tools for expressing their own thoughts.

### 4. You Try Again

**Turn feedback back into communication.**

Give the learner another meaningful opportunity to use the language that has just been introduced, corrected, or refined.

Rather than stopping when the learner understands an explanation, teachers encourage them to retrieve, adapt, and apply the language in another communicative situation.

Teacher support should gradually decrease as the learner becomes more capable of using the language independently.

**Teacher goal:** Help the learner turn something they understand into something they can use.

---

## The Hamkke Learning Cycle

The four stages are not intended to divide every lesson into four rigid blocks.

Real conversation develops naturally.

A lesson may move through the Hamkke Approach several times:

**We Talk → We Go Deeper → We Refine → You Try Again → We Talk...**

A learner expresses an idea.

The teacher helps develop it.

Useful language emerges.

The language is refined.

The learner uses it again.

Then the conversation continues.

This allows lessons to remain natural and responsive while maintaining a consistent educational purpose.

## What Must Remain Consistent

Hamkke teachers do not need to have the same personality or teach every learner in exactly the same way.

Different learners require different levels of structure, support, challenge, pacing, and interaction.

Teachers are expected to exercise professional judgment.

However, the educational principles behind the Hamkke Approach should remain recognizable across Hamkke lessons.

A Hamkke lesson should consistently aim to:

- begin with what the learner can already communicate
- create meaningful opportunities to use English
- develop the learner's thoughts rather than simply collect answers
- listen before deciding what needs to be taught
- use follow-up questions purposefully
- connect language instruction to communication whenever appropriate
- correct selectively rather than interrupting every mistake
- preserve the learner's intended meaning and individual voice
- give learners opportunities to apply useful feedback
- gradually develop greater independence in communication

The goal is not for every Hamkke teacher to teach identically.

The goal is for every learner to experience the same underlying educational philosophy:

**English becomes more useful when learners are given meaningful opportunities to use it.**

## Hamkke Teaching Principles

The following principles apply across Hamkke lessons regardless of the learner's age, level, goals, or lesson materials.

They define the educational standards behind the Hamkke Approach.

Teachers may adapt how these principles are applied, but the principles themselves should remain consistent.

---

### Principle 1: Communication Comes Before Perfection

The purpose of English is communication.

Accuracy is important, but learners should not become afraid to speak because they are trying to produce a perfect sentence before expressing an idea.

Hamkke teachers create an environment where learners are encouraged to communicate first and improve through use.

This does not mean accuracy is ignored.

It means correction should support communication rather than constantly interrupt it.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should allow learners reasonable space to complete their thoughts before correcting language unless immediate support is necessary for understanding, communication, or the learner's ability to continue.

---

### Principle 2: Listen Before You Teach

Teachers should not assume what a learner needs simply because a topic, grammar point, or vocabulary item appears in the lesson material.

Give the learner an opportunity to use English first.

Their communication can reveal:

- what they already know
- what they can use independently
- where they hesitate
- what they repeatedly struggle with
- what they are trying to express
- what language may be useful next

Teaching decisions should respond, whenever appropriate, to evidence from the learner's actual communication.

#### Hamkke Standard

Before providing unnecessary language or explanations, teachers should first consider whether the learner can express the idea independently.

---

### Principle 3: Follow the Learner's Thought

Lesson materials provide direction, but meaningful conversation does not always follow a predetermined sequence.

When a learner introduces a relevant idea, experience, question, or perspective that creates a valuable opportunity for communication, teachers may explore it further.

Completing every prepared question or activity is not more important than meaningful language use.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers may depart from prepared questions or materials when doing so creates a worthwhile learning opportunity and remains appropriate to the purpose of the lesson.

The material supports the conversation.

**The conversation does not exist simply to finish the material.**

---

### Principle 4: Ask With Purpose

Follow-up questions are a central part of the Hamkke Approach.

However, the goal is not simply to make learners speak for longer periods of time.

Questions should help learners develop communication by encouraging them to:

- explain
- describe
- clarify
- give reasons
- provide examples
- compare
- reflect
- imagine
- express opinions

The complexity of the question should be appropriate to the learner.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should respond to what the learner actually says rather than mechanically asking a predetermined sequence of follow-up questions.

Conversation should feel responsive, not like an interrogation.

---

### Principle 5: Correct Selectively

Not every mistake requires correction.

Too much correction can interrupt communication, reduce confidence, and shift the learner's attention away from meaning.

Teachers should use professional judgment to determine which language is most useful to address.

Priority may be given to language that is:

- important for understanding
- recurring
- relevant to the learner's goals
- appropriate to the learner's current level
- connected to something the learner wanted to express
- likely to be useful again
- preventing the learner from communicating clearly

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should prioritize useful feedback rather than attempting to identify and correct every error.

The guiding question is not:

**"What mistakes did the learner make?"**

It is:

**"What would be most useful for this learner to improve right now?"**

---

### Principle 6: Preserve the Learner's Voice

Hamkke helps learners express their own thoughts more effectively.

The teacher's role is not to replace a learner's simple language with unnecessarily advanced English.

Language improvements should remain realistic, useful, and appropriate to what the learner was actually trying to communicate.

For example:

A learner says:

> "I don't like big groups because many people make me tired."

A teacher could refine this to:

> "I don't really like being around big groups of people because I get tired easily."

The goal is not to transform it into language that the learner would be unlikely to understand, remember, or naturally use.

#### Hamkke Standard

Refinement should improve the learner's English while preserving their intended meaning and helping them develop language they can realistically make their own.

---

### Principle 7: Teach Language in Context Whenever Possible

Grammar, vocabulary, pronunciation, and expressions become more meaningful when learners understand why they need them.

Whenever appropriate, teachers should connect language instruction to something the learner is currently trying to communicate.

If a learner needs a word, structure, or expression to develop an idea, that moment can become a meaningful teaching opportunity.

This does not mean Hamkke avoids explicit instruction.

There are times when direct teaching, focused practice, textbooks, exercises, or structured explanation are appropriate.

The important question is whether learners can eventually connect what they learn to actual language use.

#### Hamkke Standard

Language instruction should, whenever practical, return to communication.

---

### Principle 8: Give the Learner Another Opportunity to Use It

Understanding an explanation is not the same as being able to use the language independently.

After useful language has been introduced or refined, teachers should look for appropriate opportunities for the learner to retrieve and apply it again.

This may happen through:

- another question
- a new example
- a related situation
- a short role-play
- a later part of the conversation
- another lesson

Support should gradually decrease as the learner becomes more capable.

A useful progression is:

**Teacher provides → Teacher prompts → Learner retrieves → Learner uses independently**

#### Hamkke Standard

Whenever appropriate, feedback should lead back to language use rather than ending with explanation alone.

---

### Principle 9: Adapt Support to the Learner

Personalization is more than changing the conversation topic.

Different learners may require different amounts and types of support.

Support may include:

- examples
- sentence starters
- choices
- visuals
- gestures
- modeling
- repetition
- guiding questions
- vocabulary prompts
- additional thinking time
- more challenging follow-up questions

The teacher should adjust support according to the learner's age, level, confidence, goals, and current ability.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should provide enough support for the learner to communicate successfully while avoiding unnecessary dependence on the teacher.

As the learner develops, appropriate support should gradually decrease.

---

### Principle 10: Progress Means Greater Independence

Hamkke does not define progress only by the amount of material completed or the number of grammar points and vocabulary words studied.

These can provide useful information, but meaningful progress also appears in how independently a learner can communicate.

Signs of developing independence may include:

- giving longer or more developed answers without prompting
- explaining reasons and examples independently
- retrieving previously learned vocabulary or expressions
- applying feedback in a new situation
- communicating despite not knowing every word
- asking for clarification or language when needed
- noticing and repairing some errors independently
- handling increasingly complex ideas
- relying less on teacher prompts
- expressing thoughts with greater comfort and clarity

#### Hamkke Standard

Learner progress should be considered in terms of both developing language knowledge and the learner's growing ability to use that knowledge independently.

---

### A Shared Standard, Not a Script

These principles establish what should remain recognizable across Hamkke lessons.

They do not require every teacher to:

- have the same personality
- ask the same questions
- use the same materials
- correct language in exactly the same way
- follow identical lesson timing
- conduct every conversation in the same style

Teachers are professionals and are expected to exercise sound judgment.

Hamkke provides the educational framework.

Teachers apply that framework to the learner in front of them.

The standard is therefore not identical teaching.

**The standard is a consistent educational purpose delivered through thoughtful, learner-responsive teaching.**

## Teaching Standards and Professional Flexibility

Hamkke aims to provide a recognizable and consistent learning experience without requiring teachers to conduct identical lessons.

To achieve this, teaching decisions are understood through three levels:

1. **Hamkke Required Standards**
2. **Teacher Professional Judgment**
3. **Learner-Specific Adaptation**

Understanding the difference between these levels allows Hamkke to maintain educational quality while giving teachers the flexibility needed for responsive teaching.

---

### 1. Hamkke Required Standards

Required standards represent the educational practices that should remain consistent across Hamkke.

Teachers working with Hamkke are expected to:

- follow the educational principles of the Hamkke Approach
- create meaningful opportunities for learners to use English
- listen and respond to the learner rather than simply deliver prepared material
- use follow-up questions purposefully when appropriate
- allow reasonable space for learners to complete their thoughts
- avoid unnecessary interruption and excessive correction
- provide feedback that is useful and appropriate to the learner
- preserve the learner's intended meaning when refining language
- connect learning to actual language use whenever practical
- provide opportunities for learners to apply useful feedback
- adapt support according to learner needs
- encourage increasing independence over time
- maintain a respectful and psychologically safe learning environment
- prepare appropriately for lessons
- maintain professional boundaries and conduct
- follow Hamkke policies related to lessons, attendance, learner records, feedback, and reporting

These standards define the educational experience Hamkke is responsible for providing.

A teacher's individual style may differ, but these principles should remain recognizable in their teaching.

---

### 2. Teacher Professional Judgment

Hamkke teachers are not expected to follow a fixed script.

Teachers may use their professional judgment when deciding:

- how to begin a conversation
- which follow-up questions to ask
- when a topic is worth exploring further
- when to move on from a topic
- which errors should receive attention
- whether correction should be immediate or delayed
- how explicitly a language point should be explained
- which examples will best help the learner
- when repetition or focused practice is useful
- how much time to spend on a particular activity
- when lesson materials should be followed closely
- when it is educationally useful to depart from prepared materials
- how to balance conversation and structured instruction
- which appropriate teaching techniques best suit the learner

Professional flexibility does not mean teaching without standards.

Teacher decisions should remain consistent with the purpose and principles of the Hamkke Teaching Framework.

#### Guiding Question

When making an instructional decision, teachers should be able to ask:

**"How will this help the learner communicate, understand, or use English more independently?"**

If there is a clear educational reason, the teacher has room to make that decision.

---

### 3. Learner-Specific Adaptation

Hamkke does not expect every learner to experience the same lesson in the same way.

Teaching should respond to relevant differences in:

- age
- English level
- confidence
- communication ability
- learning goals
- interests
- pace of learning
- previous learning experiences
- amount of support needed
- reason for studying English

Adaptation may affect:

- question difficulty
- conversation depth
- teacher language
- speaking expectations
- amount of thinking time
- use of visuals or examples
- amount of modeling
- correction frequency
- type of feedback
- lesson materials
- amount of repetition
- balance between structured and open conversation
- degree of teacher support

Adaptation should help the learner access the Hamkke Approach.

It should not remove the educational purpose behind it.

For example, a young beginner and an advanced adult may require very different lessons.

The young learner may use pictures, choices, sentence starters, and short follow-up questions.

The adult may explore opinions, experiences, hypothetical situations, and more precise language.

Both learners can still experience:

**We Talk → We Go Deeper → We Refine → You Try Again**

---

### The Hamkke Consistency Principle

Consistency at Hamkke does not mean that every classroom looks the same.

It means that learners should consistently experience teaching that is:

- responsive
- purposeful
- communicative
- appropriately challenging
- supportive without creating unnecessary dependence
- attentive to meaningful language development

A learner should be able to move from one Hamkke teacher to another without expecting identical personalities or lesson styles.

However, the learner should still recognize the underlying Hamkke experience.

**Different teachers. Different learners. One shared educational standard.**

## Learner Application Standards

The Hamkke Approach remains consistent across learner groups.

What changes is how the approach is applied.

Age, English level, confidence, goals, and communication ability influence the amount of support a learner needs, the complexity of conversation, the type of feedback provided, and the degree of independence expected.

The following standards provide general guidance.

They are not rigid age-based lesson templates.

Individual learners within the same age group may require very different levels of support.

---

### Young Learners

#### Educational Focus

For young learners, Hamkke aims to develop comfort using English as a means of communication while gradually building vocabulary, sentence formation, listening comprehension, pronunciation, and speaking independence.

Young learners should not be expected to produce unnecessarily long or complex answers simply to demonstrate progress.

A small increase in independent communication can represent meaningful development.

For example:

**Initial response:**

> "Pizza."

**With support:**

> "I like pizza."

**Developed response:**

> "I like pizza because it's delicious."

Each stage represents greater communicative ability.

---

#### Applying the Hamkke Approach

##### We Talk

Begin with concrete, familiar, and age-appropriate topics.

These may include:

- family
- school
- friends
- food
- toys
- games
- animals
- hobbies
- daily routines
- recent experiences
- pictures or lesson materials

Teachers may use visuals, gestures, choices, and modeling to make communication accessible.

The goal is to discover what the learner can already understand and express.

##### We Go Deeper

Help the learner add one manageable layer to the first answer.

For example:

> "I played."

may become:

> "What did you play?"

then:

> "Who did you play with?"

then:

> "Was it fun?"

then:

> "Why was it fun?"

Going deeper with a young learner does not require abstract discussion.

The goal is to help the learner gradually add information, reasons, descriptions, and simple opinions.

##### We Refine

Focus on language the learner can realistically understand and reuse.

This may include:

- useful vocabulary
- basic sentence structure
- recurring grammar patterns
- pronunciation
- natural short expressions
- expanding incomplete answers

Avoid overwhelming the learner with multiple corrections at once.

Modeling and natural reformulation may often be more appropriate than lengthy explanations.

##### You Try Again

Give the learner another simple opportunity to use the language.

This may involve:

- another picture
- another example
- a similar question
- a short game
- a new object or character
- a related personal question

Repetition may be useful, but whenever possible it should return to meaning rather than exist only as mechanical repetition.

---

#### Teacher Support

Young learners may appropriately receive:

- visual support
- gestures
- choices
- sentence starters
- teacher modeling
- repetition
- vocabulary prompts
- shorter questions
- additional processing time
- encouragement to attempt an answer before receiving the full language

Teachers should avoid immediately supplying answers that the learner may be capable of producing with appropriate support.

In an online lesson, teachers should also remain attentive to the learner's engagement and ability to participate through the screen. When appropriate, teachers may vary the pace, type of interaction, visual support, or activity while preserving the educational purpose of the lesson.

---

#### Signs of Progress

Possible signs of progress include:

- responding with less teacher prompting
- expanding beyond one-word answers
- using familiar sentence patterns independently
- remembering previously learned vocabulary
- answering familiar questions more comfortably
- giving simple reasons or examples
- asking or answering basic follow-up questions
- using English despite not knowing every word
- improving intelligibility
- showing greater willingness to communicate

---

### Teen Learners

#### Educational Focus

For teen learners, Hamkke aims to develop increasingly independent communication while helping learners express more detailed ideas, experiences, preferences, and opinions.

Lessons should respect the learner's developing maturity.

Conversation should not feel unnecessarily childish, nor should learners be pushed into adult-level abstract discussion before they are ready.

---

#### Applying the Hamkke Approach

##### We Talk

Use topics that are relevant to the learner's actual life and interests.

These may include:

- school
- friendships
- hobbies
- entertainment
- technology
- social media
- sports
- family
- future plans
- travel
- personal preferences
- age-appropriate current topics

Teachers should create space for the learner to contribute rather than turning the lesson into a sequence of teacher-led questions.

##### We Go Deeper

Encourage learners to move beyond statements and begin supporting their ideas.

For example:

> "I don't like group projects."

may develop into:

> "Why don't you like them?"

> "What usually happens?"

> "Can you give me an example?"

> "Do you think group projects can ever be useful?"

As ability develops, learners can increasingly practice explaining, comparing, supporting opinions, and considering different situations.

##### We Refine

Help learners develop more precise and natural ways to express ideas they already want to communicate.

Feedback may increasingly include:

- vocabulary choice
- natural expressions
- sentence development
- grammar patterns
- pronunciation
- organization of ideas
- clarity

Avoid replacing the learner's voice with language that sounds unnecessarily formal or advanced.

##### You Try Again

Ask the learner to apply useful language to another example, situation, or part of the conversation.

As independence develops, teacher prompting should gradually decrease.

---

#### Teacher Support

Teen learners may benefit from:

- guiding questions
- examples
- vocabulary prompts
- sentence starters when necessary
- additional thinking time
- opportunities to choose topics
- increasingly open-ended questions
- structured support for organizing longer answers

Teachers should remain attentive to confidence and avoid creating an atmosphere where every answer feels evaluated.

---

#### Signs of Progress

Possible signs of progress include:

- developing answers without repeated prompting
- explaining reasons more clearly
- giving relevant examples
- expressing opinions with greater detail
- retrieving previously learned language
- asking clarification questions when necessary
- applying feedback to new situations
- organizing thoughts more clearly
- relying less on teacher-provided sentences
- becoming more comfortable sustaining conversation

---

### Adult Learners

#### Educational Focus

For adult learners, Hamkke aims to help learners use English more independently, precisely, and comfortably in conversations relevant to their lives and goals.

Adult learners may already possess substantial passive knowledge of English.

The challenge may therefore be less about learning English for the first time and more about accessing and applying existing knowledge during real communication.

---

#### Applying the Hamkke Approach

##### We Talk

Begin with genuine communication appropriate to the learner's interests, experiences, responsibilities, or goals.

Topics may include:

- everyday life
- work
- relationships
- travel
- culture
- personal experiences
- decisions
- goals
- social situations
- professional situations
- interviews
- opinions
- reflective topics

The teacher should allow the learner's responses to influence where the conversation develops.

##### We Go Deeper

Encourage learners to explain the thinking behind their first response.

Depending on the learner, this may involve:

- reasons
- examples
- comparisons
- experiences
- consequences
- hypothetical situations
- alternative perspectives
- reflection
- clarification
- more nuanced opinions

Depth should serve communication.

A conversation does not need to become intellectually complex simply for the sake of appearing advanced.

##### We Refine

Adult learners may benefit from more precise feedback involving:

- natural vocabulary
- collocations
- expressions
- grammar
- pronunciation
- sentence structure
- tone
- clarity
- organization of ideas
- appropriateness for particular situations

Teachers should distinguish between language that is technically possible and language that is useful for the learner.

The goal is not to make every learner sound identical or unnecessarily formal.

##### You Try Again

Create opportunities for the learner to retrieve and apply refined language independently.

This may involve:

- another discussion question
- a related personal example
- a hypothetical situation
- role-play
- reformulating an earlier thought
- applying the language later in the lesson
- revisiting the language in a future lesson

As ability develops, learners should increasingly retrieve useful language without direct teacher prompting.

---

#### Goal-Specific Adult Lessons

Some adult learners may have specific communication goals such as:

- job interviews
- professional communication
- presentations
- travel
- examinations involving speaking
- relocation
- social communication

Structured preparation is appropriate when required.

However, Hamkke should avoid unnecessary dependence on memorized scripts.

Where possible, learners should understand the meaning and structure behind prepared language and practice adapting it when questions or situations change.

The goal is not only to prepare the expected answer.

The goal is to help the learner remain capable when the conversation becomes unexpected.

---

#### Teacher Support

Adult learners may benefit from:

- precise vocabulary support
- reformulation
- clarification questions
- language alternatives
- targeted grammar explanation
- pronunciation feedback
- structured speaking frameworks
- opportunities for extended speaking
- challenging but relevant follow-up questions

Support should remain appropriate to the learner's actual ability rather than assumptions based solely on age, profession, or educational background.

---

#### Signs of Progress

Possible signs of progress include:

- sustaining longer conversations independently
- developing ideas with reasons and examples
- retrieving useful language more quickly
- expressing increasingly precise meanings
- reformulating when a word or expression is unavailable
- applying feedback without direct prompting
- noticing and repairing some errors independently
- adapting language to different situations
- handling unexpected questions more comfortably
- communicating complex thoughts with greater clarity
- relying less on memorized language
- showing greater confidence navigating real interaction

---

### One Approach Across Different Learners

Young learners, teens, and adults may experience very different Hamkke lessons.

That difference is intentional.

The content, pacing, language, activities, level of support, and depth of conversation should respond to the learner.

The underlying educational cycle remains:

**We Talk → We Go Deeper → We Refine → You Try Again**

Hamkke standardizes the educational purpose.

**Hamkke personalizes the path used to reach it.**

## Lesson Delivery Standards

Hamkke lessons should feel personal and responsive while maintaining a consistent educational purpose.

Teachers are not required to follow a fixed minute-by-minute lesson structure.

Instead, they are expected to prepare appropriately, respond to the learner during the lesson, make purposeful teaching decisions, and maintain enough continuity for learning to develop across lessons.

---

### Before the Lesson

Teachers should arrive prepared to teach the learner in front of them.

Preparation may include:

- reviewing relevant learner information
- reviewing notes from previous lessons when available
- knowing the current lesson material or topic
- identifying possible communication opportunities within the material
- preparing appropriate questions, examples, visuals, or activities when needed
- being aware of relevant learner goals
- ensuring required teaching materials and technology are ready

Preparation does not require predicting exactly how the conversation will develop.

A teacher should be prepared enough to provide direction while remaining flexible enough to respond to the learner.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should understand the purpose and relevant context of the lesson before it begins.

Lesson preparation should support responsive teaching rather than create a script that must be completed.

---

### During the Lesson

#### 1. Begin With Connection

Where appropriate, begin by reconnecting with the learner.

This may involve:

- asking about something previously discussed
- checking in about the learner's day or week
- following up on a previous experience
- introducing the day's topic naturally
- beginning with an appropriate activity for a young learner

Warm-up conversation is not simply time before the "real lesson."

It can provide valuable opportunities to:

- observe spontaneous communication
- retrieve previously used language
- build continuity between lessons
- identify new learning opportunities
- help the learner become comfortable speaking

The length and form of this interaction should depend on the learner and the lesson.

#### Hamkke Standard

Opening conversation should have communicative value and should not become routine filler.

---

#### 2. Create Meaningful Learner Speaking Opportunities

Hamkke lessons should provide learners with genuine opportunities to communicate.

Teachers should avoid unnecessarily dominating the conversation or providing complete answers before learners have had a reasonable opportunity to respond.

Teacher talk is valuable when it:

- provides understandable language input
- explains something the learner needs
- models useful English
- responds naturally to the learner
- provides an example
- guides an activity
- helps move the conversation forward

Teacher talk becomes less useful when it consistently replaces opportunities for the learner to think, retrieve language, and communicate.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should create sufficient space for learners to participate actively rather than treating them primarily as listeners.

There is no fixed teacher-to-learner speaking ratio required across all Hamkke lessons.

The appropriate balance depends on the learner and the educational purpose of the moment.

---

#### 3. Use Materials as Tools

Hamkke may use textbooks, worksheets, articles, pictures, videos, discussion materials, teacher-created resources, digital activities, or other appropriate learning materials.

Materials provide structure and learning opportunities.

They do not determine the entire educational experience.

Teachers are encouraged to connect materials to meaningful communication whenever appropriate.

For example, a textbook question may become the beginning of a broader conversation.

A vocabulary item may be connected to the learner's own experience.

A reading passage may lead to explanation, comparison, reflection, or opinion.

An exercise may reveal language that needs further practice in conversation.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers are not expected to complete every page, question, or activity simply because it appears in the material.

Teachers should make reasonable decisions based on learner needs and lesson priorities.

At the same time, teachers should not repeatedly abandon structured learning materials without an educational reason.

---

#### 4. Correct Without Taking Over the Conversation

Correction should follow the principles established in the Hamkke Teaching Framework.

Teachers may use different correction techniques depending on the situation.

These may include:

- natural reformulation
- delayed correction
- explicit correction
- clarification
- prompting the learner to self-correct
- providing an alternative expression
- brief pronunciation practice
- focused explanation when necessary

The teacher should consider:

- whether the error affects understanding
- whether it is recurring
- whether the learner is ready for the correction
- whether the language is relevant to the learner's goals
- whether immediate correction will unnecessarily interrupt communication
- whether the learner can realistically use the improved language

#### Hamkke Standard

Correction should have a clear learning purpose.

Teachers should avoid turning spontaneous communication into continuous error correction.

---

#### 5. Develop the Learner's First Answer

A learner's first response is often the beginning of a learning opportunity rather than the end of one.

When appropriate, teachers should use follow-up questions to help learners:

- add information
- explain
- describe
- give reasons
- provide examples
- compare
- clarify
- reflect
- express an opinion

However, teachers should also recognize when a topic has reached its natural end.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should develop worthwhile communication rather than asking additional questions simply to prolong a topic.

---

#### 6. Create Opportunities to Reuse Language

Useful language introduced during a lesson should, whenever practical, return to communication.

A learner may be invited to:

- answer the question again
- apply the language to another example
- use it in a related discussion
- use it with another picture or situation
- use it in a short role-play
- retrieve it later in the lesson

Not every language point requires immediate repetition.

Teachers should use professional judgment to determine when another opportunity to use the language would be educationally valuable.

#### Hamkke Standard

Whenever practical, learning should move beyond explanation toward application.

---

#### 7. Respond to the Learner's State

Learner performance can vary from lesson to lesson.

A learner may be tired, distracted, unusually quiet, highly engaged, anxious, energetic, or struggling with material they normally handle well.

Teachers should remain responsive without abandoning appropriate expectations.

Possible adjustments may include:

- reducing question complexity
- increasing thinking time
- providing more support
- changing the activity
- briefly changing the topic
- using more structured practice
- providing a greater challenge when the learner is ready

#### Hamkke Standard

Adaptation should help the learner participate meaningfully.

It should not automatically remove challenge whenever the learner experiences difficulty.

---

### Lesson Timing

Hamkke lessons should not be divided into mandatory time blocks for each stage of the Hamkke Approach.

The four stages describe an educational cycle, not a timetable.

A single lesson may contain several small cycles of:

**We Talk → We Go Deeper → We Refine → You Try Again**

Some lessons may require more structured instruction.

Others may contain extended conversation.

Young learners may move between activities more frequently.

Goal-specific lessons may require focused practice.

Teachers should manage lesson time according to learner needs and lesson purpose.

#### Hamkke Standard

Lesson time should be used intentionally.

Teachers should avoid both rushing through material for the sake of completion and allowing conversation to continue without educational direction.

---

### Closing the Lesson

When appropriate, the end of a lesson should help create continuity rather than feeling like an abrupt stop.

Depending on the learner and lesson, the teacher may:

- briefly revisit useful language
- ask the learner to recall an expression
- recognize something the learner communicated well
- identify something worth continuing next time
- connect the lesson to an upcoming topic
- provide an appropriate practice suggestion

A formal summary is not required after every lesson.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should remain aware of what is worth carrying forward into future lessons.

---

### After the Lesson

Where Hamkke systems require lesson records or teacher notes, teachers should record information that is useful for educational continuity.

Useful notes may include:

- language worth revisiting
- recurring communication difficulties
- useful vocabulary or expressions introduced
- notable learner strengths
- areas requiring continued support
- topics or interests worth returning to
- relevant progress observations
- material or activities completed when necessary

Teacher notes should be factual and useful.

They should not make unsupported claims about learner progress.

#### Hamkke Standard

Lesson records should help Hamkke understand the learner over time and support continuity across lessons, reports, and teachers.

---

### Continuity Across Hamkke

A Hamkke lesson should not exist as an isolated interaction.

Over time, teachers should increasingly understand:

- how the learner communicates
- what support the learner needs
- recurring strengths
- recurring difficulties
- useful language previously introduced
- relevant goals
- areas where independence is developing

This continuity becomes especially important when producing learner Progress Reports or when another authorized Hamkke teacher needs to understand the learner's history.

The objective is not simply to deliver individual lessons.

**Hamkke should build an understanding of the learner across lessons.**


---

## Feedback and Correction Standards

Feedback at Hamkke should help learners communicate more effectively without making them afraid to communicate imperfectly.

Correction is not separate from the Hamkke Approach.

It is primarily part of **We Refine**, followed whenever appropriate by **You Try Again**.

The purpose of correction is not to identify every difference between the learner's English and perfect English.

The purpose is to help the learner notice and use language that will meaningfully improve their communication.

---

### The Hamkke Correction Principle

Before correcting a learner, teachers should consider:

**"Will addressing this now meaningfully help this learner communicate?"**

A correction may be valuable because it:

- improves understanding
- addresses a recurring difficulty
- gives the learner language they were actively searching for
- improves a useful or frequently used expression
- supports a relevant learner goal
- prevents misunderstanding
- improves intelligibility
- helps the learner express the intended meaning more accurately
- reinforces language the learner is currently developing

A mistake does not automatically require correction simply because the teacher noticed it.

---

### When Immediate Correction May Be Appropriate

Immediate correction may be useful when:

- communication has broken down
- the listener may misunderstand the intended meaning
- the learner asks whether something is correct
- the learner is actively searching for a word or expression
- a brief correction can be made naturally without disrupting the conversation
- pronunciation significantly affects intelligibility
- the lesson is currently focused on the language being practiced
- the learner is attempting to self-correct and needs support

For example:

> Learner: "Yesterday I go to the hospital."

The teacher may naturally respond:

> "Oh, you went to the hospital yesterday? What happened?"

The corrected form is modeled while the conversation continues.

---

### When Delayed Correction May Be More Appropriate

Delayed correction may be useful when:

- the learner is developing a longer thought
- interruption may cause the learner to lose the idea
- the message remains understandable
- several related errors can be addressed together
- the language would benefit from explanation
- the learner is speaking with unusual confidence or fluency and interruption would unnecessarily disrupt that communication
- the teacher wants the learner to notice or self-correct later

The teacher may remember or record the language and return to it at an appropriate point.

For example:

> "Earlier you said, 'I very like working alone.' Your meaning was clear. A more natural way to say it is, 'I really like working alone.'"

The teacher can then return the language to conversation.

---

### When Correction May Not Be Necessary

Teachers may reasonably choose not to correct an error when:

- the meaning is clear
- the error is minor relative to the learner's current level
- correcting it would provide little immediate educational value
- more important language deserves attention
- too many corrections have already been introduced
- the learner is not yet ready to use the corrected form meaningfully
- the error is unlikely to recur or affect communication
- correction would unnecessarily interrupt an important communicative moment

Choosing not to correct something is not the same as failing to notice it.

Selective correction is an instructional decision.

---

### Correction Priorities

When several possible corrections appear, teachers should prioritize rather than attempt to address everything.

A useful order of consideration is:

#### 1. Meaning and Understanding

Does the language create misunderstanding or prevent communication?

#### 2. Recurring Patterns

Is the learner repeatedly making the same error?

#### 3. Learner Need

Was the learner actively trying to express something they did not have the language for?

#### 4. Usefulness

Is the corrected language likely to be useful again?

#### 5. Learner Goals

Is the language particularly relevant to the learner's reason for studying English?

#### 6. Readiness

Can the learner reasonably understand and begin using the improved language?

#### 7. Naturalness and Precision

Would refinement help the learner express the intended meaning more naturally or accurately?

These considerations are not a mandatory ranking for every situation.

Teachers should use them to make purposeful correction decisions.

---

### Correction Techniques

Hamkke does not require one correction technique for every learner or situation.

Teachers may use different techniques depending on the educational purpose.

---

#### Natural Reformulation

The teacher naturally repeats the learner's meaning using improved language.

> Learner: "She give me good advice."

> Teacher: "She gave you good advice? What did she suggest?"

This is especially useful when the teacher wants to preserve conversational flow.

---

#### Explicit Correction

The teacher directly explains what should change.

> "We usually say 'interested in,' not 'interested about.'"

This can be appropriate when clarity is useful and the learner is ready for direct explanation.

---

#### Prompted Self-Correction

The teacher gives the learner an opportunity to notice and repair the language.

> Learner: "Yesterday I go shopping."

> Teacher: "Yesterday you...?"

> Learner: "Went shopping."

Self-correction can help learners become less dependent on the teacher.

However, teachers should avoid repeatedly prompting learners when they clearly do not know the answer.

---

#### Clarification

The teacher asks for clarification when the intended meaning is uncertain.

> "Do you mean that you were disappointed, or that you were surprised?"

This allows language development to begin with understanding what the learner actually wants to say.

---

#### Language Expansion

The teacher keeps the learner's original meaning while helping them express it more fully.

> Learner: "The movie was good."

> Teacher: "What made it good?"

The learner may develop:

> "The movie was good because the story was unpredictable."

---

#### Providing a Natural Alternative

Sometimes a learner's sentence is understandable and grammatically possible but does not sound natural in the situation.

The teacher may provide a useful alternative.

> "You can say that, but in everyday conversation you might hear, 'That really caught me off guard.'"

Teachers should avoid presenting personal stylistic preferences as absolute language rules.

---

#### Pronunciation Feedback

Pronunciation feedback should prioritize intelligibility and useful communication.

Teachers may focus on:

- sounds that affect understanding
- word stress
- sentence stress
- rhythm
- frequently used words
- pronunciation patterns relevant to the learner

The goal is not to eliminate the learner's accent.

A learner does not need to sound like a native speaker in order to communicate successfully.

#### Hamkke Standard

Pronunciation instruction should aim for clearer, more comfortable, and more intelligible communication rather than accent elimination.

---

### Protect the Learner's Meaning

Before reformulating a learner's sentence, the teacher should understand what the learner intends to communicate.

Teachers should avoid turning a simple learner statement into sophisticated language that changes its tone, personality, or meaning.

The objective is:

**Learner's thought → clearer or more useful English**

not:

**Learner's thought → teacher's preferred way of speaking**

This distinction is especially important when working with intermediate and advanced learners who are developing their own voice in English.

---

### Avoid Correction Overload

A learner may produce many correctable forms during spontaneous speech.

Attempting to address all of them can make feedback difficult to remember and discourage communication.

Teachers should select a manageable amount of feedback according to:

- learner level
- lesson purpose
- importance of the language
- learner confidence
- cognitive load
- likelihood of reuse

It is better for a learner to meaningfully understand and use a small number of valuable improvements than to receive a long list of corrections they cannot apply.

---

### Feedback Should Return to Use

Whenever appropriate, correction should not end with:

> "Do you understand?"

Understanding is useful, but Hamkke aims for application.

After refining language, the teacher may:

- ask the learner to express the idea again
- ask a related question
- provide a new situation
- revisit the language later
- prompt the learner to retrieve it independently

This completes the movement from:

**Notice → Understand → Use**

and supports the final stage of the Hamkke Approach:

**You Try Again.**

---

### Positive Feedback

Feedback at Hamkke is not limited to correcting errors.

Teachers should also notice meaningful strengths.

Useful positive feedback may recognize:

- successful communication
- independent language retrieval
- clearer explanation
- effective use of a previously learned expression
- successful self-correction
- improved pronunciation
- a more developed answer
- greater independence
- successful communication despite missing vocabulary

Positive feedback should be specific whenever practical.

Instead of only saying:

> "Good job."

a teacher might say:

> "You explained that without needing my help this time."

or:

> "You used the expression from our last lesson naturally there."

This helps learners understand what they are actually doing well.

---

### Feedback Across Lessons

Not every language issue needs to be solved within one lesson.

Some patterns should be observed across multiple lessons before conclusions are made.

Teachers should distinguish between:

- a one-time mistake
- an emerging difficulty
- a recurring pattern
- a developing skill
- a demonstrated improvement

This distinction becomes important when recording learner observations and preparing Progress Reports.

#### Hamkke Standard

Formal progress statements should be based on observable patterns rather than isolated moments whenever possible.

---

### Feedback Standard

Hamkke feedback should be:

- purposeful
- selective
- understandable
- appropriate to the learner
- connected to communication
- respectful of the learner's intended meaning
- manageable enough to apply
- followed by opportunities for use when appropriate

The purpose of feedback is not to show learners everything that is wrong with their English.

**The purpose is to help them discover what they can do next with it.**


---

## Learner Progress Standards

Hamkke views learner progress as the development of both English knowledge and the ability to use that knowledge meaningfully and increasingly independently.

Progress is not defined only by:

- lessons completed
- textbook pages finished
- vocabulary memorized
- grammar points studied
- test scores

These may provide useful information, but they do not fully describe a learner's ability to communicate.

Hamkke therefore considers progress through observable changes in how learners understand, express, develop, refine, and independently use English over time.

---

### The Hamkke View of Progress

A learner may be making meaningful progress when they become increasingly able to:

- understand and respond with less support
- express ideas more clearly
- develop answers beyond the first response
- give reasons, examples, descriptions, or explanations
- retrieve useful language more independently
- apply previously introduced language in new situations
- communicate despite gaps in vocabulary
- clarify or reformulate when communication becomes difficult
- notice and repair some errors independently
- use increasingly appropriate vocabulary and expressions
- communicate with greater intelligibility
- handle more complex ideas
- rely less on teacher prompting
- participate more comfortably in spontaneous interaction

Not every learner will develop in these areas at the same rate.

Progress should be interpreted in relation to the learner's starting point, goals, age, level, and learning context.

---

### Progress Areas

For internal observation and future Progress Reports, Hamkke organizes communicative development into the following areas.

These areas are not intended to function as rigid test categories.

They provide a shared language for understanding learner development across Hamkke.

---

#### 1. Communication and Expression

This area considers how effectively the learner communicates their intended meaning.

Teachers may observe:

- ability to respond to questions
- ability to express personal thoughts
- ability to describe experiences
- ability to explain ideas
- ability to communicate when exact vocabulary is unavailable
- clarity of intended meaning
- willingness to attempt communication

##### Possible Development

A learner may move from:

> giving isolated words or very short responses

toward:

> communicating complete ideas with increasing clarity and independence.

---

#### 2. Answer Development

This area considers the learner's ability to move beyond an initial response.

Teachers may observe whether the learner can:

- add details
- explain reasons
- provide examples
- describe situations
- compare ideas
- clarify meaning
- express opinions
- reflect when appropriate to their level

##### Possible Development

A learner may move from:

> "It was fun."

toward:

> "It was fun because I went with my friends, and we tried something new."

The objective is not simply longer answers.

Development should reflect greater ability to communicate meaningful information.

---

#### 3. Language Range

This area considers the learner's developing ability to use vocabulary, expressions, and sentence structures appropriate to what they want to communicate.

Teachers may observe:

- vocabulary range
- use of useful expressions
- ability to choose increasingly precise words
- sentence variety
- ability to retrieve previously introduced language
- ability to express increasingly complex meanings

##### Possible Development

A learner may move from repeatedly relying on familiar basic language toward having more options for expressing similar or more precise meanings.

---

#### 4. Accuracy and Language Control

This area considers the learner's developing control of grammar, sentence structure, word forms, and other language patterns.

Hamkke does not expect error-free communication.

Teachers should consider:

- recurring grammar patterns
- whether errors affect meaning
- consistency of familiar structures
- ability to apply previous feedback
- ability to self-correct
- increasing control during spontaneous communication

##### Possible Development

A learner may first use a corrected form only after teacher support, then begin using it with prompts, and eventually use it independently.

Progress therefore may appear as:

**Teacher provides → Teacher prompts → Learner self-corrects → Learner uses independently**

---

#### 5. Vocabulary and Expression Use

Knowing a word is different from being able to retrieve and use it during communication.

Teachers may observe whether learners:

- understand useful vocabulary
- retrieve vocabulary during conversation
- use new expressions appropriately
- reuse language from previous lessons
- choose more precise language
- paraphrase when a word is unavailable

##### Possible Development

Progress may appear when a learner begins using previously introduced vocabulary without waiting for the teacher to provide it again.

---

#### 6. Pronunciation and Intelligibility

Hamkke evaluates pronunciation primarily according to communication.

Teachers may observe:

- whether speech can be understood
- recurring sounds that affect intelligibility
- word stress
- sentence stress
- rhythm where relevant
- pronunciation of frequently used language
- ability to notice and adjust pronunciation after feedback

The goal is not accent elimination.

Different accents are compatible with effective English communication.

##### Possible Development

Progress may appear when the learner becomes easier to understand, produces important words more consistently, or adjusts pronunciation with less teacher support.

---

#### 7. Interaction and Conversation Skills

Communication involves more than answering questions.

Teachers may observe the learner's developing ability to:

- maintain a conversation
- respond appropriately to another speaker
- ask questions
- ask follow-up questions
- request clarification
- clarify their own meaning
- react naturally
- take conversational turns
- introduce relevant information
- remain engaged when the conversation develops unexpectedly

Expectations should remain appropriate to the learner's age and level.

##### Possible Development

A learner may move from primarily responding to teacher questions toward contributing more actively to the direction of the conversation.

---

#### 8. Independence

Independence is a central indicator of progress within the Hamkke Approach.

Teachers may observe how much support the learner requires to:

- begin an answer
- continue speaking
- retrieve vocabulary
- build a sentence
- explain an idea
- apply feedback
- correct an error
- handle an unfamiliar question
- communicate around missing language

##### Possible Development

A learner may progress through stages such as:

**Needs direct support → Responds with prompts → Uses occasional support → Communicates independently**

The goal is not to remove teacher support completely.

The goal is for support to become more targeted as the learner becomes increasingly capable of doing more for themselves.

---

### Progress Is Not Always Linear

Learner performance naturally varies.

A learner may communicate very well in one lesson and struggle in another because of:

- unfamiliar topics
- fatigue
- hesitation or reduced confidence in the moment
- difficulty of the language
- increased complexity
- limited background knowledge
- temporary difficulty retrieving language

One weaker lesson does not necessarily indicate regression.

Likewise, one unusually strong performance does not necessarily demonstrate established improvement.

#### Hamkke Standard

Progress judgments should be based on patterns observed across time whenever possible.

---

### Distinguishing Observation From Progress

Teacher observations and progress claims are not the same thing.

An observation describes what happened.

For example:

> "The learner answered several questions using complete sentences during today's lesson."

A progress statement makes a comparison across time.

For example:

> "The learner is increasingly answering questions in complete sentences without needing sentence starters."

The second statement requires evidence from more than one point in time.

#### Hamkke Standard

Teachers should not convert isolated observations into claims of improvement without reasonable evidence of development across lessons.

---

### Evidence of Progress

Evidence may come from:

- lesson observations
- teacher notes
- learner language produced during lessons
- repeated use of previously introduced language
- reduced need for teacher support
- increased complexity or clarity of communication
- successful application of feedback
- relevant lesson activities
- assessments when used
- learner work when appropriate
- patterns observed across an enrollment

No single source of evidence is required for every learner.

The type of evidence should remain appropriate to the learner and program.

---

### Progress Relative to the Learner

Hamkke does not compare learners against one another when describing individual progress.

Progress should primarily be considered relative to:

- the learner's previous performance
- the learner's starting point
- the learner's goals
- the level of support previously required
- the communication demands appropriate to the learner

This allows progress reporting to remain personal and meaningful.

---

### Avoiding Unsupported Progress Claims

Teachers should avoid statements such as:

> "The learner's grammar has greatly improved."

unless there is sufficient evidence to support the claim.

More useful reporting identifies what has actually been observed.

For example:

> "The learner is using past-tense forms more consistently when describing recent experiences, although occasional prompting is still needed."

This communicates both development and the learner's current support needs.

#### Hamkke Standard

Progress reporting should be specific, evidence-based, and balanced.

Hamkke should never invent improvement in order to make a report sound positive.

---

### Recognizing Strengths

Progress reporting should not focus only on difficulties.

Teachers should identify meaningful learner strengths, including abilities that may already have been present at the beginning of the reporting period.

Examples may include:

- clear pronunciation
- strong listening comprehension
- willingness to communicate
- effective use of context
- ability to explain ideas
- strong vocabulary retrieval
- thoughtful responses
- effective self-correction
- confidence asking questions

A strength does not need to be newly developed in order to be worth recognizing.

Teachers should distinguish between:

**Current strength**

and

**Demonstrated improvement**

when reporting.

---

### Areas for Continued Development

Progress Reports should also identify useful next areas of focus.

These should be:

- relevant to observed learner needs
- realistic for the learner's current stage
- specific enough to guide future lessons
- connected to communication whenever possible

For example:

Instead of:

> "Needs to improve grammar."

Hamkke may report:

> "Continued practice with past-tense forms will help the learner describe experiences more consistently during spontaneous conversation."

The second statement explains why the development area matters.

---

### Progress Across an Enrollment

Hamkke Progress Reports should consider development across the enrollment or reporting period rather than functioning as a summary of one recent lesson.

Where evidence is sufficient, reports may describe:

- current strengths
- demonstrated development
- recurring challenges
- level of independence
- useful language patterns
- areas for continued development
- recommended next focus

Not every category must show improvement during every enrollment.

A truthful report may identify areas that remain stable or continue to require support.

---

### The Hamkke Progress Principle

Hamkke does not measure progress only by asking:

**"What has this learner studied?"**

We also ask:

**"What can this learner now do with English?"**

Meaningful progress is increasingly visible when learners can take knowledge that once required explanation, modeling, or prompting and begin using it for themselves.

**At Hamkke, progress means not only knowing more English, but becoming increasingly able to use English independently and meaningfully.**


---

## Teacher Records and Documentation Standards

Hamkke maintains learner records to support educational continuity, informed teaching decisions, Progress Reports, and consistency across the company.

Documentation should be useful without creating unnecessary administrative work for teachers.

The purpose of lesson documentation is not to record everything that happened during a lesson.

The purpose is to preserve information that may meaningfully help Hamkke understand and support the learner over time.

---

### Documentation Principles

Teacher documentation should be:

- concise
- factual
- educationally relevant
- based on actual observation
- useful for future lessons
- understandable to another authorized Hamkke teacher when necessary
- appropriate for future progress reporting

Teachers should avoid writing notes simply for the sake of completing a record.

A short, meaningful observation is more useful than a long note containing little educational information.

---

### Lesson Records

Where Hamkke systems require a lesson record, teachers should record information that is useful for continuity.

Depending on the lesson, this may include:

- topic or material covered
- useful vocabulary or expressions introduced
- recurring language difficulty
- notable communication strength
- language that may need to be revisited
- level of support required
- successful use of previous feedback
- relevant pronunciation observation
- notable change in learner independence
- topic or activity worth continuing
- relevant learner goal discussed during the lesson

Not every lesson needs observations in every area.

Teachers should record what is educationally meaningful.

---

### Observation vs. Interpretation

Teachers should distinguish between what they directly observed and what they infer from it.

For example:

#### Observation

> "Needed sentence starters to explain why she preferred the first option."

#### Interpretation

> "Still benefits from support when explaining reasons spontaneously."

Both may be useful.

However, interpretations should remain reasonably supported by what the teacher has observed.

Teachers should avoid unsupported assumptions about:

- motivation
- personality
- effort outside lessons
- intelligence
- home environment
- emotional state
- reasons for learner performance

unless the learner or an appropriate guardian has directly provided relevant information and recording it is necessary for educational purposes.

---

### Recording Strengths

Teacher records should not function only as error logs.

Teachers should also record meaningful strengths when they provide useful information about the learner.

Examples include:

> "Explained her opinion clearly without prompts."

> "Remembered and used 'used to' from the previous lesson."

> "Pronunciation was clear throughout the reading."

> "Asked a follow-up question independently."

> "Used context successfully when she did not know the exact word."

These observations can later help Hamkke identify patterns of strength and development.

---

### Recording Difficulties

When documenting difficulties, teachers should be specific.

Avoid:

> "Bad grammar."

Prefer:

> "Frequently shifts between present and past tense when describing past experiences."

Avoid:

> "Vocabulary is weak."

Prefer:

> "Often knows the idea she wants to express but needs teacher support to retrieve more precise vocabulary."

Avoid:

> "Cannot answer independently."

Prefer:

> "Currently needs follow-up prompts to develop answers beyond the first sentence."

Specific observations are more useful for future teaching and progress reporting.

---

### Recording Teacher Support

The amount of support a learner requires can provide important evidence of development.

Where relevant, teachers may note whether the learner:

- required direct modeling
- responded with sentence starters
- responded after a vocabulary prompt
- responded after a guiding question
- self-corrected after a prompt
- retrieved language independently
- applied feedback independently

This can help Hamkke identify changes that may not be visible from accuracy alone.

For example:

> "Previously needed the full sentence model. Today used the structure after only a brief prompt."

This provides stronger evidence of development than simply writing:

> "Improved."

---

### Recording Language

Teachers do not need to transcribe learner speech extensively.

However, short examples of learner language may be useful when they demonstrate:

- a recurring pattern
- an important correction
- successful application
- a meaningful before-and-after example
- language worth revisiting

For example:

> Original: "I very like it."

> Refined: "I really like it."

or:

> Used "I used to..." independently when discussing childhood.

These examples can provide useful evidence for future reports.

---

### Continuity Between Lessons

Before teaching a learner, teachers should have reasonable access to relevant previous information where Hamkke systems provide it.

Teachers are not expected to reread an entire learner history before every lesson.

However, recent or important records may help the teacher:

- revisit useful language
- follow up on a previous topic
- recognize recurring difficulties
- continue unfinished material
- reduce unnecessary repetition
- recognize developing independence

#### Hamkke Standard

Documentation should help lessons feel connected over time rather than functioning as unrelated individual sessions.

---

### Documentation Across Teachers

As Hamkke grows, a learner may occasionally study with another authorized Hamkke teacher.

Records should therefore be written with enough clarity that another teacher can understand relevant educational context without relying on private conversations with the previous teacher.

Documentation should focus on the learner's educational needs and lesson history rather than personal judgments about the learner.

#### Hamkke Standard

Learner records belong to Hamkke's educational system and should support responsible continuity of service.

---

### Preparing for a Progress Report

A Progress Report should not require the teacher to reconstruct an entire enrollment from memory.

When preparing a report, relevant lesson records should help the teacher identify:

- repeated strengths
- demonstrated development
- recurring difficulties
- changes in the amount of support required
- useful language development
- communication patterns
- areas that remain inconsistent
- appropriate next priorities

One observation alone should not automatically become a progress claim.

Teachers should look for patterns across the reporting period.

---

### Documentation and Progress Claims

The following distinction should remain clear:

#### Lesson Observation

> "Used two previously introduced expressions independently today."

#### Emerging Pattern

> "Has begun retrieving previously introduced expressions with less prompting."

#### Demonstrated Progress

> "Across recent lessons, the learner has increasingly retrieved and applied previously introduced expressions independently."

The strength of the statement should reflect the strength of the available evidence.

#### Hamkke Standard

The wording of learner records and reports should never be more certain than the evidence supports.

---

### Documentation Efficiency

Hamkke should design its systems so teachers can record useful observations efficiently.

Teachers should not be expected to produce a detailed narrative after every standard lesson.

Where possible, Hamkke systems may combine:

- attendance records
- lesson information
- short teacher notes
- structured observation fields
- optional language examples
- Progress Reports

The documentation system should support teaching rather than compete with teaching time.

---

### Professional and Respectful Records

Teacher notes should be written with the understanding that they are part of a professional educational record.

Avoid insulting, dismissive, overly emotional, or unnecessary personal descriptions.

Instead of:

> "She was lazy today."

record the observable situation when educationally relevant:

> "Needed more prompting than usual and completed fewer speaking attempts during today's lesson."

Instead of:

> "He doesn't care about English."

record what can actually be observed:

> "Participated minimally in today's speaking activities despite several prompts."

Teachers should describe what they know rather than speculate about why it happened.

---

### Record Only What Is Necessary

Learner records should contain information relevant to Hamkke's educational service.

Teachers should avoid recording unnecessary private information simply because it was mentioned during conversation.

Personal information may naturally arise in conversational lessons.

That does not mean every personal detail belongs in the learner's permanent educational record.

#### Hamkke Standard

Record what Hamkke needs to teach and support the learner responsibly.

Do not turn natural conversation into unnecessary personal documentation.

---

### The Hamkke Documentation Principle

Documentation exists to preserve educational understanding.

A useful record should help answer at least one of these questions:

**What should Hamkke remember about this learner?**

**What may help the next lesson?**

**What pattern may matter over time?**
